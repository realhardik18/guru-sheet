Chapter 1: Force and Pressure

1.1 Force — A Push or a Pull

In our daily life we push, pull, kick, lift, throw and squeeze objects. A child pushes a cart in the market. A farmer pulls a plough. A goalkeeper stops a moving ball. In each of these situations, something is being moved, stopped or changed in shape. In science, all such pushes and pulls are given a single name: force.

A force cannot be seen. We only know that a force is acting because of what it does. When you push a door, you do not see the force, but you see the door swing open. When a footballer kicks a ball, you do not see the force, but you see the ball fly across the field. Force is therefore known by its effects.

An important idea to understand is that at least two objects must interact for a force to come into play. A force does not exist by itself. When you push a wall, your hand is one object and the wall is the other. When a magnet attracts a pin, the magnet is one object and the pin is the other. There is no such thing as a single object exerting a force on nothing.

1.2 Forces Acting Together

Very often, more than one force acts on an object at the same time. Consider a heavy box being pushed by two students standing on the same side. Both are pushing in the same direction. In this case the two forces add up, and the net force on the box is the sum of the two.

Now imagine the two students standing on opposite sides of the box, pushing towards each other. The forces now act in opposite directions. Here the net force is the difference between the two forces, and it acts in the direction of the larger force. If both students push equally hard, the two forces cancel out completely. The net force is zero and the box does not move at all, even though both students are working hard.

This tells us something important: the strength of a force alone does not decide what happens. The direction of the force matters just as much. A force is described completely only when we state both how strong it is and which way it acts.

1.3 What a Force Can Do

The effects of force can be grouped into a small number of clear categories.

First, a force can make a stationary object move. A ball lying still on the ground begins to roll when it is kicked.

Second, a force can stop a moving object. A goalkeeper catching a ball applies a force that brings it to rest.

Third, a force can change the speed of a moving object. Pedalling harder on a bicycle makes it speed up; applying the brakes makes it slow down.

Fourth, a force can change the direction of a moving object. A batsman striking a ball sends it off in a completely new direction, sometimes without changing its speed very much at all.

The first four effects are together described as changing the state of motion of an object. The state of motion is described by the speed of the object and the direction in which it moves. A change in either one, or in both, means the state of motion has changed. An object at rest is considered to be in a state of motion with zero speed.

Fifth, and separately, a force can change the shape of an object. Pressing a lump of dough flattens it. Stretching a rubber band makes it longer. Squeezing a plastic bottle dents its sides. In these cases the object may not move from its place at all, yet a force is clearly acting, because its shape has changed.

1.4 Contact Forces

Forces are broadly divided into two families, depending on whether or not the objects need to touch.

Muscular force is the force applied by the muscles of our body. We use muscular force to lift a bag, to open a drawer, to write, and even to breathe. Animals also use muscular force: bullocks pull carts, horses pull carriages, camels carry loads. Muscular force requires direct contact with the object being moved, and so it is a contact force.

Friction is a second contact force. When a ball rolls along the ground, it slows down and eventually stops, even though nobody is touching it. The force responsible is friction, which acts between the surface of the ball and the surface of the ground. Friction always acts to oppose the motion of an object. It comes into play whenever two surfaces are in contact and one tends to slide over the other. Friction is the reason we can walk without slipping, and it is also the reason machines wear out and need oiling.

1.5 Non-Contact Forces

Some forces act without any contact at all. These are non-contact forces, and they act at a distance.

Magnetic force is exerted by a magnet on iron, and by one magnet on another. A magnet attracts an iron pin from a small distance away without touching it. Two magnets brought close to each other may attract or repel: like poles repel one another, and unlike poles attract one another.

Electrostatic force is the force exerted by a charged body on another charged or uncharged body. A plastic comb rubbed on dry hair will pick up small pieces of paper from a distance. The comb becomes charged by rubbing, and it attracts the paper without touching it.

Gravitational force is the force with which the Earth pulls everything towards itself. A ball thrown upwards comes back down. Water flows downhill. A leaf detached from a branch falls to the ground. This pull of the Earth is called gravity, and it acts on every object, whether the object is moving or at rest.

Gravitation is not a property of the Earth alone. Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a gravitational force. The Sun holds the planets in their orbits by gravitation, and the Moon is held in its orbit around the Earth in the same way. We notice the Earth's pull most because the Earth is enormously massive compared with the objects around us.

The weight of an object is the force with which the Earth attracts it. Because weight is a force, it is measured in newtons, not in kilograms.

1.6 Pressure

Push a drawing pin into a board with your thumb. The pin goes in easily. Now try to push the blunt head of the same pin into the board with the same effort. It does not go in at all. The force is the same in both cases. What differs is the area over which that force acts.

The force acting on a unit area of a surface is called pressure.

Pressure = Force / Area

The SI unit of force is the newton, written N. The SI unit of area is the square metre, written m². Therefore the SI unit of pressure is newtons per square metre, N/m², which is given the special name pascal, written Pa. One pascal is the pressure produced when a force of one newton acts on an area of one square metre.

This relationship explains a great many everyday observations. Knives, needles, nails and axes are all given sharp edges or fine points. A sharp edge has a very small area of contact, so the same muscular force produces a very large pressure, and the object cuts or pierces easily. A blunt knife has a larger area of contact, produces less pressure for the same effort, and does not cut well.

The opposite principle is used when we want to reduce pressure. The handles of bags and suitcases are made broad so that the weight of the bag is spread over a larger area of the hand, reducing the pressure and the discomfort. School bags have wide straps for the same reason; a thin string would cut into the shoulder. The foundations of buildings are made wide so that the great weight of the building is spread over a large area of ground and the building does not sink. Camels have broad feet, which spread their weight over a large area of sand so they do not sink while walking in a desert. Tractors and army tanks have very wide tyres or continuous tracks so that they can move over soft ground without sinking in.

1.7 Pressure Exerted by Liquids and Gases

Liquids and gases also exert pressure. A liquid exerts pressure not only on the bottom of its container but also on the walls of the container.

If a tall vessel is filled with water and small holes are made in its side at different heights, water spurts out of every hole. The water from the lowest hole travels the farthest, and the water from the highest hole travels the least. This shows that the pressure exerted by a liquid increases with depth. The deeper you go, the greater the height of the liquid column pressing down, and the greater the pressure.

This is why the walls of a dam are built much thicker at the bottom than at the top. The bottom of the dam has to withstand far greater water pressure than the top. It is also why divers going to great depths must wear specially designed suits, and why submarines are built with thick, strong hulls.

Gases exert pressure on the walls of their container in the same way. Air filled into a balloon presses outward on the rubber in all directions, which is why a balloon inflates into a rounded shape rather than a flat one. When we inflate a bicycle tyre, the air inside pushes outward on the tube in every direction.

1.8 Atmospheric Pressure

The Earth is surrounded by a layer of air called the atmosphere, extending many kilometres above its surface. Air has weight, and the pressure exerted by this column of air is called atmospheric pressure.

The atmospheric pressure at sea level is very large — roughly the weight of a considerable mass pressing on every square centimetre of our bodies. We are not crushed by it because the pressure of the blood and fluids inside our bodies pushes outward and balances the atmospheric pressure pushing inward.

A simple demonstration shows atmospheric pressure clearly. Take a rubber sucker and press it against a smooth surface. The air between the sucker and the surface is pushed out. Atmospheric pressure now acts on the outer surface of the sucker and holds it firmly in place. It is difficult to pull the sucker off because there is no air underneath to push back.

Atmospheric pressure decreases as we go higher above sea level, because there is less air above us pressing down. This is why mountaineers climbing high peaks carry oxygen cylinders, and why aircraft cabins must be pressurised.

Summary of Key Ideas

A force is a push or a pull, and requires an interaction between at least two objects.

Forces have both magnitude and direction. Forces in the same direction add; forces in opposite directions subtract.

A force can change the state of motion of an object, change its shape, or both.

Contact forces include muscular force and friction. Non-contact forces include magnetic force, electrostatic force and gravitational force.

The force acting per unit area is called pressure, calculated as force divided by area, and measured in pascals.

Reducing the area of contact increases pressure; increasing the area of contact reduces pressure.

Liquids and gases exert pressure on the walls of their containers, and the pressure exerted by a liquid increases with depth.

The pressure exerted by the atmosphere is called atmospheric pressure, and it decreases with increasing altitude.
